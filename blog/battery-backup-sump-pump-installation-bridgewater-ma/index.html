Battery Backup Sump Pump Installation in Bridgewater, MA

Storms that raise the water table often knock out the power too. A backup pump keeps the pit working when the main pump cannot.

By the Bridgewater Express Plumbing team. Published November 14, 2026.

Call (508) 389-4045

The short answer

A battery backup sump pump is a second pump with its own battery, charger and float switch. It sits in the same pit, a little higher than the main pump, and starts if the main pump fails or the power goes out. Without one, a power outage in a storm leaves the pit with no pump.

Backup types

TypeHow it worksLimits
Battery backupA 12 volt battery runs a DC pump through a controllerRuntime depends on battery size and how often the pump runs
Water-powered backupUses house water pressure to pump out waterNeeds municipal water pressure, so it is not for private wells
GeneratorPowers the main pump during an outageNeeds a transfer setup and fuel
Second AC pumpA spare pump on the same powerHelps if one pump fails, not if power fails

For most homes on town water, a battery backup is the practical choice. A generator also works if you already own one.

How to size a backup

  • ✓

    How often the main pump runs in a heavy storm.

  • ✓

    How fast the pit fills, which sets the pump capacity you need.

  • ✓

    How long outages last in your area, which sets the battery size.

  • ✓

    How high the discharge needs to lift the water.

  • ✓

    Whether the pit is large enough for two pumps and floats.

A small battery that runs for a short time is better than nothing, and a larger one is better in a long storm. We size it to your pit.

What an installation includes

  1. We check the pit, the main pump and the discharge line.

  2. We set the backup pump on a stand or bracket slightly above the main pump.

  3. We add a check valve, a separate float and a connection to the discharge.

  4. We mount the battery and controller above the floor, with an alarm.

  5. We test both pumps by lifting the floats and simulating a power loss.

Ask about the alarm. A beep that tells you the main pump failed or the battery is low is worth the extra cost.

Maintenance that keeps it working

Test the pumps every few months by pouring water into the pit until the float lifts. Check the battery terminals for corrosion, and keep the charger plugged in. Most makers suggest replacing the battery every few years, and a dead battery is the most common reason a backup fails.

Clear the pit of silt and debris once or twice a year. A float that sticks on debris will not start. If the pump keeps cycling, see basement waterproofing for why.

Mistakes that leave a backup useless

  • ✓

    Letting the battery age past its life without testing it.

  • ✓

    Plugging the charger into an outlet that is on the same tripped circuit.

  • ✓

    Mounting the backup float lower than the main float, so it fires first or never.

  • ✓

    Using the same discharge pipe without a separate check valve.

  • ✓

    Never lifting the floats to confirm that both pumps start.

A backup that has never been tested is a hope, not a system. A five minute test every few months proves it.

Setting up two pumps in one pit

PartMain pumpBackup pump
Float heightLower, starts firstSlightly higher, starts if water keeps rising
PowerHouse current12 volt battery and charger
DischargeOwn pipe and check valveOwn pipe or a tee with a second check valve
AlarmNone or a high-water alarmAudible alarm for failure or low battery

Separate check valves keep the two pumps from pushing water back through each other. Ask how your installer plans to do it.

Water that keeps coming in despite the pumps

A sump system moves water that reaches the pit. If a pump runs constantly in a storm, the pit may be overwhelmed, the pump may be undersized or the water table may be very high. More pumping power helps, but so does fixing where the water enters.

That is where basement waterproofing and drainage come in. A system that collects water at the footing and sends it to a well-sized pit performs better than a pump alone. We size the pit, the pump and the backup as one system.

Caring for the battery

A backup battery is a deep-cycle battery that sits on a charger for years and then must perform in a storm. Its life is limited, and a battery that tests fine in spring may be weak by the next winter.

Check the charger light each season and test the system by lifting the float or pouring in water until the backup runs. Replace the battery on the maker's schedule, not when it fails.

Add a high-water alarm if your system lacks one. A loud alert in the middle of a storm gives you time to act, whether that means moving belongings, running a second pump or calling a plumber.

If the power goes out for a long stretch, a battery lasts only for hours of pumping, not days. A generator or a larger battery bank is the next step for a house that floods during outages. We can talk through what size matches your pit and how often the main pump runs in a storm.

Check the permit and the discharge rules

Bridgewater's Building Department says plumbing work tied to a building project needs a separate permit, and it runs an electronic permit system. Whether a sump pump or a backup install needs a permit depends on the job, so ask the department.

Where the discharge goes also matters. A line that dumps at the foundation sends water back to the pit. Ask your plumber to run it away from the house, and avoid discharging onto a neighbor's lot or into the sanitary sewer.
