How to Plumb a Tub and Shower in Bridgewater, MA

A tub and shower look simple from the outside. Behind the wall, there is a drain, a vent, a mixing valve and a supply that have to be right before the tile goes up.

By the Bridgewater Express Plumbing team. Published November 14, 2026.

Call (508) 389-4045

The short answer

A tub or shower needs three things: a drain with a trap and a vent, hot and cold supply lines to a valve and a way to test everything before the wall is closed. The order matters. The drain goes in first, the valve second and the test and inspection before drywall.

The order of work

  1. Frame the opening and mark the drain location from the plans.

  2. Set the drain and trap, and connect it to the vent and the branch drain.

  3. Mount the valve at the right depth for the finished wall thickness, with blocking behind it.

  4. Run hot and cold supply lines to the valve and to the shower arm or spout.

  5. Test the supply with pressure and the drain with water, then call for the rough inspection.

The drain slope, the trap size and the vent distance are the points an inspector checks. Get them right before the wall closes.

The parts and what they do

PartPurposeCommon mistake
Trap and drainHolds a water seal and carries waste awayWrong size or too little slope
VentLets air in so the trap does not siphonVent too far from the trap
Mixing valveBlends hot and cold and limits scald riskSet too deep or too shallow for the trim
Supply linesBring water to the valve and the outletNo shutoffs or poor supports
BlockingWood behind the valve and fixturesForgotten, so the trim does not hold

A tub drain is typically a smaller pipe than a shower drain, and local code sets the sizes. Do not guess at them.

Decisions to make before the work starts

  • ✓

    Tub, shower pan or a curbless shower, since the drain and the floor framing change.

  • ✓

    Pressure-balance or thermostatic valve, which affects the temperature stability.

  • ✓

    Where the valve sits, and whether one valve or two will serve multiple outlets.

  • ✓

    Access panel behind the wall on the other side, for later service.

A good access point saves a wall later. It is a small extra in the plan that pays for itself the first time a valve fails.

Water heater and pressure considerations

A big rain head or body sprays can use more hot water than a small tank can supply. Check the water heater capacity before you pick the fixtures, and look at the flow limits on the label. A new shower may be the moment to look at water heater installation options.

Water pressure affects spray and valve choice. If your pressure is high or low, tell us up front so we pick a valve that works.

Mistakes that show up after the tile is up

  • ✓

    A valve set at the wrong depth, so the trim will not seat or leaves a gap.

  • ✓

    No test of the supply lines before the wall was closed.

  • ✓

    A drain that was not checked for slope, so water pools and drains slowly.

  • ✓

    No blocking behind the valve or the shower arm, so they loosen.

  • ✓

    No access to the valve, which turns a cartridge change into a tile repair.

Every one of these is cheap to fix before the wall closes and costly after. That is the case for a careful rough-in and a rough inspection.

Questions to settle before you buy fixtures

QuestionWhy it matters

One outlet or several?More outlets need more supply and a diverter
What is the water heater capacity?Large spray fixtures drain a small tank quickly
Who supplies the fixtures?Fixture dimensions set the rough-in measurements

Buy the valve and the trim before the rough-in. The valve maker's chart sets the depth, and a plumber cannot set it right without the actual part.

Protecting the finished space

A tub or shower is the wettest place in the house. A small leak in the wall can go unseen for months, staining the ceiling below and rotting framing. A pressure test of the supply lines and a flood test of the drain before the wall closes are cheap insurance.

After the finish work, run the shower and check the ceiling and walls beneath and beside it for dampness. If you see a stain later, leak detection finds where it comes from without opening more of the wall than needed.

Why the drain and vent matter as much as the valve

A tub or shower drain needs the right slope, a trap and a vent. Without a vent, water drains slowly and gurgles, and the trap can lose its water seal, which lets sewer gas into the room.

Pipe sizes and fixture distances are set by the plumbing code. In Massachusetts that means the Uniform State Plumbing Code, 248 CMR 10, and the local inspector checks the rough-in before the walls close.

That is why we treat a new tub or shower as a small system, not a fixture swap. Getting the drain and vent right at the start is far cheaper than opening finished tile to fix them.

Permit and inspection steps in Bridgewater

Bridgewater's Building Department says plumbing or gas work tied to a building project needs a separate permit alongside the building permit. For single-family homes it lists a rough plumbing inspection that must pass before the rough frame inspection, and a final inspection that includes the final plumbing.

That matters for a tub or shower because the rough inspection happens before the walls close. Ask your plumber to schedule it. The town says a permit card must be in hand before work begins and that missing it can mean a triple permit fee.
